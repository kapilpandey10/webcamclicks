The Evolution of Photo Booths: From 1925 Photomaton to Korean 4-Cut Culture

By the WebcamClicks Editorial Staff · Updated October 2026 · 12 min read

Overview: Long before Instagram selfies and TikTok filters, human beings craved a private sanctuary where they could drop a coin behind a velvet curtain, strike four uninhibited poses, and receive an instant strip of physical memories. Here is the 100-year history of the photo booth phenomenon.

1. 1925: Anatol Josepho and the Broadway Photomaton Revolution

In September 1925, a Siberian-born immigrant named Anatol Josepho opened a small storefront at 1659 Broadway in New York City. Inside stood his patented invention: the Photomaton.

For twenty-five cents, customers climbed into an enclosed cabinet with an automated coin slot. Over eight minutes, an internal chemical darkroom exposed positive paper directly, developed the photographic emulsion in heated chemical baths, dried the strip, and dispensed eight miniature portraits. In its first six months, Josepho's Broadway studio drew over 280,000 visitors, with lines stretching three blocks down Times Square.

2. 1960s: Andy Warhol and Photo Booths as Fine Art

During the early 1960s, pop artist Andy Warhol transformed commercial arcade photo booths into a defining medium of contemporary portraiture. Warhol was fascinated by the machine's absolute impartiality: a photo booth did not flatter, direct, or judge its subjects.

Warhol would take artists, models, and socialites into 42nd Street arcade booths, hand them quarters, and instruct them to pose through dozen-strip bursts. These mechanical film strips became the foundation for iconic silkscreen masterpieces, including his historic 1963 commission Ethel Scull 36 Times.

3. 1995: The Japanese Purikura Craze

In the mid-1990s, game developer Sasaki Miho conceived an invention aimed squarely at teenage girls who loved trading idol stickers. In 1995, Atlus and Sega released Print Club (abbreviated as Purikura) across Japanese video arcades.

Unlike Western analog passport booths, Purikura was revolutionary: it introduced digital touchscreen stylus doodling, colorful frame borders, digital cosmetic skin-softening, and instant adhesive sticker sheets. Purikura transformed photo booths from utilitarian ID generators into collaborative social rituals.

4. The Modern Renaissance: Korean "Life4Cuts" (인생네컷)

Over the past five years, South Korea spearheaded a worldwide photo booth renaissance through brands like Life4Cuts (인생네컷), Photoism, and Haru Film. These modern retail spaces are bright, self-service studios stocked with quirky headbands, oversized glasses, and high-end DSLR strobe lighting.

The output is the iconic vertical 4-cut aesthetic strip: four sequential snapshots framed in minimalist pastel or monochrome cardstock. It bridges the tactile nostalgia of analog film strips with modern Gen-Z social media aesthetics.
